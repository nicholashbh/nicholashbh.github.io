Can I return to clinical practice if I leave?

Yes, but the route is narrower the later you leave. Your registration with the Singapore Medical Council is unaffected by leaving a residency: you remain a registered medical practitioner throughout the sixty months, and you can return to a non-training clinical post as a Medical Officer at any point. What changes is your access to another residency.

MOH Holdings is explicit on that second question. A candidate who withdraws from a residency without exceptional grounds cannot apply again in the next cycle, and a doctor involuntarily separated from residency or specialist training cannot reapply at all. Any new residency starts at Residency Year 1 regardless of how far you had progressed, so time served here is not credited forward.

In practice the decision is easier in Years 1 to 3, where you are still doing clinical postings and your skills are current, than in Years 4 and 5, where two years of senior residency have taken you a long way from the ward. If you are seriously weighing an exit, put it to the Programme Director early rather than at the point of leaving.

How does this compare with Internal Medicine or Family Medicine?

The unit of care is a population rather than a person. Internal Medicine and Family Medicine treat the patient in front of you, and the result of the decision arrives inside the consultation or the admission. Preventive Medicine works on the conditions that produce those consultations — Nutri-Grade labelling on beverages, the national childhood immunisation schedule, permissible noise exposure limits at a worksite, food hygiene grading — and the result arrives years later, usually without a face attached to it.

Both are full-time specialist training. The trade is reach in exchange for immediacy and clinical continuity, and it is worth being honest with yourself about which of those you want.

Do my prior Medical Officer years let me skip R1?

No. This one now has a definite answer. MOH Holdings states that “all candidates are expected to enter Residency training at Residency Year 1 (R1) regardless of prior experience”, and separately that no pre-selection or off-cycle commencement is allowed. NUHS says the same thing in the same words. There is no advanced standing mechanism to apply for.

There is a floor, though, in the other direction: the Specialists Accreditation Board states that the residency accepts candidates only from Postgraduate Year 2 onwards, and MOH Holdings requires you to be at least PGY2 by the July you enter. So prior service does not shorten the programme, but a minimum amount of it is required before you can start.

Time spent as a Medical Officer still counts for something. It is what makes you credible at interview and useful in the work. It just does not shorten the sixty months.

Am I eligible as a conditionally registered international medical graduate?

On registration category, yes. The Specialists Accreditation Board states that at entry a resident must have completed Postgraduate Year 1 and hold “a valid Conditional or Full Registration with Singapore Medical Council”. Conditional registration is explicitly sufficient.

But registration is not the binding constraint. MOH Holdings states that graduates from overseas “must secure an offer of employment as a doctor from MOHH before they are eligible to apply”, and that you must already be an MOH Holdings employee at the point of application. Employment comes first and the residency application second. Placement is also prioritised for Singapore citizens, permanent residents and locally-trained foreigners.

One point worth raising directly with the Programme Office: the Board allows international medical graduates to enter part-way through, up to Year 1 of senior residency, but that route is exercised rarely and only on documented equivalent training assessed by the Residency Advisory Committee. Passing postgraduate examinations is not equivalent to structured training on its own, and applicants coming through the ordinary MOH Holdings match enter at Residency Year 1.

Do I need an MPH before applying?

No. The Master of Public Health is part of the programme, not a prerequisite for it. Applying with no formal public health qualification is the ordinary case and it is what most residents do.

If you already hold one, it may count. The Specialists Accreditation Board states that the degree “may be obtained before commencing the residency (subject to RAC’s approval)” — so an existing Master of Public Health can satisfy the requirement rather than being read again, provided the Residency Advisory Committee approves it. It does not shorten the sixty months.

If you are heading for the Occupational Medicine pathway, note the Board’s warning that residents interested in it “must ensure they take the appropriate modules to satisfy specialist accreditation standards”. A degree taken before you joined may not contain them.

Is the MPH full-time or part-time?

Either, and that is a more useful answer than the site previously gave. The Specialists Accreditation Board sets out three routes: before commencing the residency, during it as a part-time student, or with a break of service as a full-time student — usually at an overseas school. All three need Residency Advisory Committee approval. Dr Sean Lim, one of the residents profiled on the alumni page, read his at Columbia University.

The usual route is part-time alongside your postings, so you are carrying coursework and a rotation in the same week. Be aware that NUS states some classes run full days, from 8.30am to 6pm, and that part-time students have to take leave from work to attend them. The degree is 50 units in total — 26 core, 24 elective — and it is not offered by distance learning.

Passing it is a gate rather than an ornament: junior residency does not end without it. Most residents take the coursework across R1 and R2, finishing the dissertation in R3.

Do I still do clinical work, and how much?

Yes, but it is not the bulk of your week. Clinical competence is one of the programme's selection criteria and stays under assessment throughout, so clinical practice is retained rather than dropped. The realistic picture is that most of your five years is spent outside a ward — on policy, surveillance, evaluation, regulation and programme work — and that is the point of the specialty rather than a defect in it.

As a guide: junior residency carries roughly four to six months of direct patient care a year, served in the outbreak management posting, the clinical preventive medicine attachments and up to fifty-two half-day primary healthcare sessions. Senior residency carries less — typically one to two months a year, and on the Occupational Medicine pathway that time is spent in workplace and fitness-to-work clinics rather than on a ward.

What are the on-call and weekend commitments?

It varies by posting, and the spread is wide. A ministry or statutory board rotation does not carry the roster of a healthcare cluster or a Singapore Armed Forces posting, and an outbreak response can rearrange a week without notice.

Typically: ministry, statutory board and agency postings run office hours with no call. Cluster and hospital postings carry roughly one to two calls a month, with occasional weekend cover. Singapore Armed Forces and Home Team postings add a standby roster during exercises and operations. Across all postings, residents may be activated for public health emergency response — during an outbreak activation that takes precedence over the scheduled rotation, and the hours are not fixed in advance.

If this is your deciding factor — and for applicants with young children it often is — ask a current resident what the roster looked like last month, not what it looks like on paper.

Can I locum or moonlight?

Yes, with approval. Residents are employed through the Sponsoring Institution and are bound by its rules on outside clinical work. Locum and other paid external work requires written approval from the Programme Director in advance, and it must not conflict with your rotation duties, your call roster or your Master of Public Health coursework. Approval is not usually withheld where those conditions are met.

Ask before you take a shift rather than after. The answer is administrative rather than difficult, but it has to be on file.

What if I miss the interview registration window?

It depends which window. For the July 2027 intake the eResidency portal closes first, on 24 September 2026, and MOH Holdings states in terms that it “WILL NOT be possible to re-open or extend the application window”. Interview registration with NUHS stays open until 2 October 2026 — eight days longer.

So: if you missed the portal, you have missed the cycle, and the next intake is July 2028. If you submitted in time but missed interview registration, that is recoverable — MOH Holdings’ own guide tells late applicants to email the programmes directly and ask whether slots remain. Do that rather than assuming.

How many places are there each intake?

Four or five. Across the last eight intakes the programme admitted 35 residents in total, the smallest cohort three and the largest five. The number is not fixed year to year. Whatever else this means for you, plan on a cohort small enough that you will know every member of it by name.

What happens if I do not pass a progression review?

You do not enter senior residency until the gate is cleared. Under the current training requirements junior residency ends at three of them: passing the Master of Public Health, passing the Progression Preventive Medicine Competency Committee Review, and completing the Primary Healthcare Systems Project — a three-thousand-word report, proposed on one page and approved by the Competency Committee before you start it. All three are required, and clearing two of the three is not progression.

You will see it coming. The Competency Committee reviews your portfolio at least once each rotation and meets you every six months for an oral discussion of your progress, reporting to the Residency Advisory Committee after each six-month rotation. Where progress is not satisfactory the Committee recommends remedial action and follow-up monitoring.

What follows a failure is handled case by case. Two published facts bound it: the Residency Advisory Committee may require a further period of training beyond the minimum or terminate the residency altogether, and your total candidature is sixty months plus a further thirty-six — exit certification must be obtained inside that window. In practice a resident who does not clear a gate is placed on a remediation plan agreed with the Competency Committee and reassessed at the next six-month review; a second unsuccessful attempt is referred to the Residency Advisory Committee.

Can I switch between the Public Health and Occupational Medicine pathways?

The one published fact is where the two separate: the Specialists Accreditation Board states that the Occupational Medicine track differentiates at R4 and R5. Before that point both pathways are assessed against the same nine Public Health competencies, and the Occupational Medicine competencies are not assessed at the R3 progression review at all. So for the first three years you are not on a track that needs switching.

What that means practically is that the choice bites in senior residency, through which rotations you take and which competencies you are assessed against. If Occupational Medicine is a live option for you, the Board’s warning about the Master of Public Health matters early: take the appropriate modules, because you cannot retrofit them at R4.

The pathway is declared formally at the R3 progression review, on the Competency Committee's recommendation. It can be changed after that point only with Residency Advisory Committee approval, and a change made during R4 will normally add six to twelve months to training because the advanced rotations have to be re-planned.

What does the exit assessment consist of?

The examination itself is sat in two parts, in two different years. Part 1, in R4: sixty case-based short-answer questions, and a written paper on data interpretation and policy or programme translation. Part 2, in R5: a prepared presentation on translational public health and occupational medicine, and a portfolio-based viva. Your eligibility to sit it is decided by the Examination Committee’s review of the portfolio you have kept since R1.

Around the examination sit three further requirements: rotations at two or more sites in different organisations, six months minimum at each; a first-author paper in a peer-reviewed journal; and two mandatory courses — the Singapore Medical Association’s course on medical ethics, professionalism and health law, and the Academy of Medicine’s Geriatric Medicine Modular Course.

The paper is the requirement applicants underestimate. It must be submitted to a journal by the time you apply for the exit examination. You may sit the examination before it is accepted, with two years from the examination date for acceptance or publication — miss that and you re-sit the examination once the paper is accepted.

What do graduates do afterwards?

Public health and occupational health posts in government and in the healthcare clusters — but the specifics belong on the alumni page rather than in a generalisation here. Of 38 graduates since 2011, 36 have been traced to a current post. Four residents are named publicly in the programme brochure, and they are residents rather than graduates: Dr Nicholas Hang (2025 cohort), Dr Sean Lim (2024), Dr Yin Zhien (2022) and Dr Leonara Liu (2018).

For a sense of the national picture rather than this programme’s: the Singapore Medical Council’s register recorded 134 Public Health specialists and 41 Occupational Medicine specialists as at 31 December 2024. Of the Occupational Medicine specialists, 29 were in the public sector and 12 in the private sector. Those are counts for the whole specialty in Singapore, not for graduates of this programme.
