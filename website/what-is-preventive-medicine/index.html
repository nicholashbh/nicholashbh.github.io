The decision that prevents a disease is made years before anyone falls ill.

A specialty with no clinic list and no discharge summary. Here are the five questions a sceptical Medical Officer should ask first.

While clinical medicine treats the patient in front of you, preventive medicine changes the conditions that decide who ends up in front of you at all.

How is it different from clinical practice?

Not less medical. The unit of decision changes, and everything downstream of that changes with it.

A clinician decides what to do about the person in the room. A preventive medicine physician decides what to do about everyone who has not come in yet, including the people who never will, because the decision worked.

That single shift moves five things at once: what you are treating, how long you wait to find out whether you were right, where you sit while you wait, what counts as having done the job, and who has the standing to overrule you. The list below is the honest version of that trade, not the recruiting version.

Unit of care
A defined population with a denominator — a workforce, a birth cohort, everyone who buys a bottled drink. The individual case still matters, but as evidence rather than as the unit of decision.
Timescale
Years to decades. A change made to an immunisation schedule shows up in notification data long after the officer who argued for it has moved to another posting.
Where you work
Ten rotation sites across eight organisations: ministries, statutory boards, uniformed services and all three healthcare clusters. How much of the five years is spent in clinical settings is set out further down this page.
What success looks like
A rate that falls. An outbreak that stays small. An exposure limit nobody breaches. Success in this specialty is an absence, and absences are rarely thanked.
Who you answer to
A committee, a director, a permanent secretary, a regulator, and eventually the public record. Not one patient and one family.

This contrast is the programme's own characterisation of the specialty, written for prospective applicants. It is not a formal definition of the discipline. Confirm the wording with the Programme Director before publication.

What would I actually do?

Four instruments a Singaporean meets every week. None of them is a treatment; each is the kind of thing this specialty exists to produce.

An instrument is a rule, a schedule, a limit or a grade. Building one runs from data, through a threshold that has to survive consultation and legal drafting, to an evaluation design agreed before the rule takes effect. The domains named beneath each example are the ones that work draws on.

Nutri-Grade, 2022

Beverages sold in Singapore carry a graded mark based on sugar and saturated fat content, and the lowest-graded drinks may not be advertised. The medicine in it is the threshold: where the line between grades sits decides whether the measure changes what people drink or merely annoys them. Draws on Counting Numbers (Epidemiology and Quantitative Methods), Doing Things Right (Health Policy, Law and Ethics) and Making Things Happen (Evaluation Sciences).

National childhood immunisation schedule

The schedule sets which vaccines are given, at what age, and which of them are compulsory. Revising it means weighing new evidence against the coverage already achieved, and against the cost of changing a system that parents, polyclinics and general practitioners already follow without thinking. Draws on Whole and Sum of Parts (Health Systems), Counting Numbers (Epidemiology and Quantitative Methods) and Beyond Borders (Global and International Health). Date of the current revision.

Permissible noise exposure limits

A number written into workplace regulation, deciding how long a worker may be exposed at a given sound level and what the employer must do past that point. It has to be defensible as science, enforceable by an inspector standing on a factory floor, and survivable by the industry it binds. Draws on Health at Work (Occupational and Environmental Health), Counting Numbers (Epidemiology and Quantitative Methods) and Doing Things Right (Health Policy, Law and Ethics). Date of the current limit and the regulation it sits in.

Hawker centre hygiene grading

A grade displayed at the stall, turning an inspection record into something a customer can act on in two seconds. The hard part is not the inspection. It is choosing a scale that shifts stallholder behaviour without ending a livelihood over one bad morning. Draws on Telling Stories (Social Science and Qualitative Methods), Making Things Happen (Evaluation Sciences) and Whole and Sum of Parts (Health Systems). Date of the current grading scheme and the agency that administers it.

Nutri-Grade, the national childhood immunisation schedule, permissible noise exposure limits and hawker hygiene grading are real Singapore instruments, cited here as examples of what the specialty produces. They are not claimed as projects of this programme or of its residents.

What if I change my mind?

The question almost every Medical Officer asks privately, and almost no residency answers in writing. Here it is answered as far as we can, and marked as missing where we cannot.

Three years into this programme you will not have done a procedure in a long time. That is the risk and it has a plain name: de-skilling. Cannulation, airway skills, the pattern recognition that comes from seeing forty patients a day — those decay, and they decay faster than knowledge does. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling the programme rather than describing it.

What matters for your decision is not whether de-skilling happens. It is how reversible your position stays while it does: whether you remain registered and practising, how many months of clinical work each training year actually contains, and whether another residency would still take you and at which year. Those are the rows below. We do not hold verified figures for them, so they are published as missing rather than estimated.

Registration during training
Whether residents hold full or conditional registration with the Singapore Medical Council throughout the sixty months, and under whose supervision.
Practising certificate
Whether a practising certificate is held and renewed across all five years, and what clinical activity is required to renew it.
Clinical months, R1 to R3
Months of direct patient care in each of R1, R2 and R3, stated as months out of twelve, and the settings they are served in.
Clinical months, R4 to R5
Months of direct patient care in each of R4 and R5, stated as months out of twelve, and whether the figure differs between the Public Health and Occupational Medicine pathways.
Re-entry to another residency
Whether a resident who leaves in R1, R2 or R3 can enter another residency programme, at which year they would enter, and whether any completed time or the Master of Public Health is credited.
If you leave the programme
Sponsorship ends from the date you leave, and fees already paid are not recovered. Liquidated damages are pro-rated against the unserved portion of the obligation. The two-year additional service obligation is served after specialist accreditation, and runs consecutively to any existing MOH or PSC bond.

The people who can answer the first five rows from experience are the residents living them, not the Programme Office. Two current residents take questions from prospective applicants with nobody from the programme copied in; their details are in the contact block at the foot of this page.

Registration, practising-certificate and clinical-exposure figures are held by the Sponsoring Institution and the Programme Office and have not been released for publication. They are shown as missing rather than estimated, because an applicant who plans an exit route on an invented figure is worse off than one who plans on none. Bond terms as published on the home page. Verify every row against the bond instrument and the Sponsoring Institution's registration policy.

What would I learn?

Eight domains carry the curriculum. The plain-English line is the programme's own; the discipline name beneath it is what the work is called everywhere else.

  • Whole and Sum of PartsHealth Systems
  • Making Things HappenEvaluation Sciences
  • Using Technology and DataDigital Informatics
  • Beyond BordersGlobal and International Health
  • Doing Things RightHealth Policy, Law and Ethics
  • Counting NumbersEpidemiology and Quantitative Methods
  • Telling StoriesSocial Science and Qualitative Methods
  • Health at WorkOccupational and Environmental Health

The eight domains are methods. They are applied across eight topic areas, which is where the subject matter lives.

  • Infectious Disease
  • Ageing and Palliative Care
  • Chronic Disease
  • Mental Health
  • Food and Nutrition
  • Maternal and Child Health
  • Primary and Community Care
  • Genomics and Precision Medicine

Eight domains and eight topic areas as set out in the programme brochure. The Master of Public Health at NUS Saw Swee Hock School of Public Health is taken during junior residency and is fully sponsored; Occupational Health is one of the available specialisations. Confirm which domains are assessed at the Progression Review and which at exit.

Where does it lead?

Two exit pathways, and a set of employers you will already have worked inside before you finish.

The programme has two exits: specialist registration in Public Health, or specialist registration in Occupational Medicine. Because the five years are spent across ten rotation sites in eight organisations, most residents reach R5 having already worked alongside the people who decide their next post. Of 38 graduates since 2011, 36 have been traced to a current post.
